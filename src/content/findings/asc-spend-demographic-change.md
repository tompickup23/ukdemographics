> **Correction, 12 October 2026.** The figures in this piece came from a
> 25-authority file typed into the site's source by hand in April 2026, with no
> raw file or script behind it, and they could not be traced to any published
> table. The file gave spend per head of total population, and it gave figures
> for district councils, which run no adult social care. The piece said the five
> councils with the fastest demographic change averaged **£612** per head, the
> five with the slowest **£472**, and that spend ran from **£442** (Ribble Valley)
> to **£724** (Blackpool) across 25 authorities. On the published figures, gross
> current expenditure on adult social care per resident aged 18 and over in
> 2024-25 is £692 in Blackburn with Darwen, £687 in Lancashire, £522 in West
> Northamptonshire and £884 in Blackpool, against £627 for England (Department of
> Health and Social Care, Adult social care finance report, England: 2024 to
> 2025, Table 14, divided by ONS mid-2025 population estimates). Pendle, Preston
> and Burnley in the first group, and all five councils in the second, are
> Lancashire districts whose adult social care is run by Lancashire County
> Council. The comparison the headline rests on therefore does not hold: on real
> figures the two groups are mostly the same council.

**What the published figures show.** Adult social care is run by upper-tier
councils: counties, unitary authorities, metropolitan districts and London
boroughs. A district council has no adult social care budget, so a district has
no spend figure of its own.

Of the five councils this piece named as changing fastest, Blackburn with Darwen
spent **£692** per adult in 2024-25 and West Northamptonshire **£522**. Pendle,
Preston and Burnley are Lancashire districts. Of the five named as changing
slowest, Wyre, Ribble Valley, Fylde, West Lancashire and Rossendale are all
Lancashire districts. Lancashire County Council spent **£687** per adult. Blackpool
spent **£884**, and England as a whole **£627**.

These figures count gross current expenditure on adult social care in the
financial year, divided by residents aged 18 and over. They are spend per adult
resident, not per person receiving care.

**Methodological limitation:** A comparison of a handful of councils is
descriptive, not causal. Spend per adult follows deprivation, the share of
residents aged 65 and over, unpaid care and local policy, so differences between
councils are not a measure of need or efficiency on their own.

Whether immigration-driven demographic change increases or decreases future ASC demand depends on the age profile of incoming populations. Younger migrant populations may temporarily reduce the 65+ share, but eventually age into the care system themselves.
